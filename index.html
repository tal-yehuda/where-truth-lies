What this project is

  • It circles one question: is mathematics discovered or invented?
  • The realist (Platonist) says the objects are out there — numbers and structures exist independently, and we uncover truths about them.
  • The formalist says it's symbols moved by rules — meaning is a layer we add, and the same system can take more than one reading.
  • The site doesn't pick a winner. Each chapter hands the same result to both sides and lets you feel the pull of each.

One thread runs through all five chapters. Mathematics runs forwards — from axioms to theorems — and its sharpest limits (Gödel, Turing) show up when a system turns that machinery on itself. The last chapter runs the machine backwards: hand it a record of output and it works out the rules that could have produced it. Every chapter is a hands-on version of the idea. You don't read about it, you play it.

Project Proposal: Syntax vs Semantics

This project is an interactive website that explores how mathematical rules (syntax) relate to human interpretation and meaning (semantics). It focuses on computability and the foundations of mathematics through five interactive chapters. A guiding principle of this work is the deliberate use of the tension between different interpretations of mathematics (such as formalist and realist perspectives) as a framework for exploration rather than resolution.

Each chapter uses a specialized sandbox to ground abstract ideas in concrete mechanics. The MIU string-rewriting system introduces the nature of formal systems. A logical calculator illustrates Gödel's incompleteness results in an accessible way. A Turing machine simulator demonstrates the limits of formal computation and specification. The Game of Life explores how complex patterns emerge from simple rules and how interpretation depends on the observer. Finally, a physics simulation connects these ideas to the role of modeling in scientific understanding.

Overall, the project uses hands-on interaction to help users reflect on how meaning and interpretation arise from formal structures.

About the Project

Goals and Main Outcomes

The main goal of this project is to make difficult concepts in mathematics easier to understand, and help develop philosophical thinking and reasoning in the context of mathematics. It covers topics like Gödel's Incompleteness, Turing's Halting Problem, and the tension between Formalism and Realism. The final product is a digital learning tool that uses interactive games to help users easily grasp the limits of mathematical systems, ultimately asking them to consider where mathematical truth actually lies.

Design Choices

The main design choice for this project is "experience first, interpretation second." Each of the five chapters requires the user to interact with a specific formal model—a string-rewriting game, a logical calculator, a Turing machine, a cellular automaton, and a physics simulation—before reading the essays explaining them. This hands-on structure is meant to clearly show the difference between blindly following mechanical rules and assigning higher-level meaning to those rules.

Challenges & AI Contributions

The main intellectual challenge when writing the content was navigating the tension between different philosophies without falling into extremes—such as sliding into pure Platonism when discussing Gödel, or pure Formalism when discussing reverse mathematics. It requires effort to keep an open mind when dealing with such absolute mathematical boundaries, but that effort is what produced the even-handed treatment we aimed for throughout the site.

On the technical side, AI tools were used to help write the frontend code (HTML, CSS, and JavaScript), fix bugs in the interactive sandboxes, and clarify the text. However, we made all final design and conceptual decisions to ensure the project met its educational goals. We are especially proud of the original reverse-mathematics game, which works as both a sandbox and a philosophical analogy, along with the Gödel proof unroller and logical calculator — building them is what finally made Gödel's reasoning click for us.

Truth, Proof, and the Illusion of Mathematical Existence

Two Ways to Read the Same Proof

The Platonist reads Gödel as pointing at a completed mathematical world, where every statement already has a fixed status with or without us. It is a serious position, and it earns its keep: it explains why doing mathematics feels like discovery, and why people working from different directions keep landing on the same results. What it adds is a commitment the proof never delivers on its own — that a fully determinate mathematical universe is really out there. That step is a choice, not a theorem.

For the formalist, that missing truth was never promised. A formal system is a machine for deriving consequences from axioms — not a window onto some pre-existing world of facts. So an undecidable sentence isn't a truth the system failed to reach; it's simply a sentence the axioms don't settle, one you can add either way. "Incompleteness" only stings if you expected the system to capture an outside truth in the first place. The formalist never did — so for them it isn't a wound at all, just an accurate map of what these particular axioms do and don't fix.

Neither reading is forced on you by the mathematics. Build the sentence yourself below, then decide which reading you find you're already using.

    Where Gödel Actually Leaves Us

    The Platonist reading is clean: we can see that G is true, the system can't prove it, so truth reaches past mechanical proof.

    The formalist leans on one word in that sentence. The Gödel numbering you just ran is bookkeeping — it never handed the system a notion of truth. So the real question is: does the system contain a truth predicate at all, or only a predicate for provability?

    Analyzing G From the Outside

    Take it structurally. If system A is consistent, G can't be proved. And ¬G can't be proved either — a proof of it would make A inconsistent.

    So G doesn't follow from the axioms at all. It is strictly independent. Add G as a new axiom and you get a consistent universe; add ¬G instead and you get an equally consistent one.

    That doesn't make truth arbitrary. It means the axioms simply don't fix a truth-value for every sentence. Inside the bare syntax there is only derivability; truth appears the moment you pick a model to read the symbols in.

    G is independent — neither it nor ¬G can be derived. So you get to extend the system. Which axiom do you add?

    When we step outside and call G "true," we're reading its symbols in the standard whole numbers — the model we grew up inside. That move is exactly what splits mechanical derivability from truth-in-a-structure. Both are real. They're just not the same thing.

    Incompleteness as a Staircase

    The branch you took runs sideways — G or ¬G. There is also a way up. Gödel's proof is carried out in Peano Arithmetic (PA), the standard axioms for the whole numbers, so PA is our base level. PA can't prove its own consistency — the statement Con(PA), a blind spot just like G — so add that as a new axiom. Each level is simply the level below plus one new axiom: PA, then PA + Con(PA), then that system plus its consistency, and on up. Every level is stronger, and every level has a fresh blind spot of its own.

    Every level is a genuinely stronger theory: it proves everything below it, and its own consistency besides. The blind spot never closes — but it never traps you either. This is the formalist's reply to "truth beyond proof": not a single fixed realm hovering above the system, but an endless, ordered tower of systems, each one seeing a little further than the last.

    Gödel doesn't just prise truth apart from proof. He pins down the shape of the gap: no system rich enough to describe itself can also fully contain its own truth. It can reflect on itself — that is what let you build G in the first place — but it can't close the loop.

    So the theorem proves neither side. It hands Platonist and formalist the same fact and lets each furnish it. And you've now handled it both ways: you chose a branch, and you climbed a level. For the formalist, that is the whole point — a blind spot is not where the mathematics ends. It's where the systems multiply.

    The short version. Gödel opens a gap between what is true and what is provable, and hands both camps the same fact. The realist reads it as truth outrunning the rules: G really is true, out there, even though no derivation reaches it — a sign that mathematics is discovered, not just generated. The formalist reads it as structure, not shortfall: inside a bare system there is only what can and can't be derived, and "true" is a label we add from outside, so incompleteness is simply what any self-describing system must run into. One theorem, two honest readings — this chapter is built to let you feel the pull of each.

    Computability

    The Limit of Algorithms

    From Formal Systems to Machines

    In the previous sections, we treated mathematics as a formal system: a collection of symbols together with rules that determine which strings may be transformed into which others. Proofs were viewed not as discoveries of meaning, but as legal sequences of symbolic manipulations.

    Computability extends the idea. Instead of asking which strings a system can derive, it asks which tasks a purely mechanical procedure can carry out. To pin that down, Turing built a device stripped to the bone: the Turing machine.

    The Turing Machine

    A Turing Machine is an idealized computational device consisting of an infinite tape divided into cells, a read/write head, a finite set of internal states, and a collection of rules.

    At every step, the machine reads the current symbol, consults its transition rules, writes a symbol, changes state, and moves left or right.

    Nothing in this description involves intelligence, understanding, creativity, or meaning. A Turing Machine simply follows rules.

    From a formalist angle, that's the point. A Turing machine is the same kind of object as the formal systems you've already met. A proof system rewrites formulas; a Turing machine rewrites tape. Different alphabet, same move.

    Read this way, computation is derivation under another name — and the payoff isn't a shrug, it's a bridge. The limit that stops proof turns out to be the same limit that stops computation (both come from self-reference, as the next section shows), and the machine running your software is the same kind of engine that grinds out theorems. Meaning enters only when someone reads the output as numbers, images, predictions, or answers.

    The Question of Decidability

    Once computation is viewed as mechanical rule-following, a natural question arises: Can every well-defined question be answered by a mechanical procedure?

    If a problem is clearly stated and precise enough, shouldn't there exist some sufficiently clever method for solving it? Computability theory revealed that this intuition is false. Some questions are not merely difficult. They are undecidable. No algorithm can answer them in all cases.

    The Halting Problem

    Suppose we are given a program (P) and an input (I). We ask: Will program (P) eventually stop when run on input (I), or will it continue forever?

    At first glance it appears reasonable. Yet Alan Turing proved that no universal algorithm can correctly answer it for every possible program and input. No matter how sophisticated the algorithm becomes, there will always exist programs for which its prediction fails.

    Why The Halting Problem Appears

    The proof resembles Gödel's incompleteness theorem. Both constructions exploit self-reference.

    Machine D (Input: Itself)

    Imagine a machine H that perfectly solves the Halting Problem.
    Now construct a new program D:

    1. Run H on Machine D.
    2. If H says "D will Halt" → Enter infinite loop!
    3. If H says "D runs forever" → Halt immediately!

    You are H. D is about to run on itself — make your prediction:

    What happens when D is given itself as input?

    Any answer produces a contradiction. The assumption that H exists must therefore be false. The obstacle appears when a system attempts to mechanically predict the behavior of systems like itself.

    The Missing Philosophical Tension

    A visitor will naturally think: "Fine. No Turing machine can solve the Halting Problem. But I can still imagine such a machine."

    But notice what has actually been ruled out. The machine is not physically impossible. The machine is not logically contradictory. You can describe it perfectly: Input a program and an input. Output HALTS or DOES NOT HALT. Always correct.

    The description is perfectly meaningful. Yet no Turing machine can instantiate it. So what is the ontological status of this thing?

    The Platonist Response

    A Platonist is often comfortable saying: The machine is impossible as a Turing machine, but the function it computes still exists. The halting relation itself is perfectly well-defined. For every program P and input I, there is a fact of the matter. The inability of a Turing machine to determine all such facts does not eliminate the facts themselves. From this perspective, computability theory is not discovering the limits of truth. It is discovering the limits of a particular method of accessing truth. The Platonic realm contains more than algorithms can reach.

    The Formalist's Counterquestion

    The formalist immediately asks: In what sense does this "machine" exist? Not: Can we build it? but: What does existence mean here? The machine cannot be specified as a Turing machine. It cannot be generated by the formal system being discussed. So what exactly is being asserted when someone says: "It exists." Is existence being confused with description? Is the ability to imagine an object enough to grant it ontological status?

    The Human Problem

    This leads to an even stranger question. Humans routinely discuss objects that no Turing machine can realize. We discuss halting oracles, infinite sets, arbitrary real numbers, and complete truth predicates. The concepts themselves appear perfectly understandable. Yet the systems we study cannot generate them.

    So where did the concepts come from?

    The Platonist sees this as evidence that human thought is reaching toward a genuine mathematical reality. The fact that we can meaningfully refer to non-computable objects suggests that mathematics is not exhausted by computation. The oracle cannot be computed. Yet it can be understood. For the Platonist, understanding is not identical to computation. The mind is capable of referring to structures that no algorithm can fully capture.

    The formalist is less willing to grant this conclusion. The formalist asks: Have we actually understood the oracle? Or have we merely attached a label to a gap in our formal machinery? Saying "A machine that always solves the Halting Problem" may not represent genuine understanding. It may merely be a placeholder. After all, one can also say: "A machine that answers every question." The sentence is grammatically meaningful. That does not mean the object itself has been coherently specified.

    So the formalist treats the oracle not as a discovered entity but as a boundary marker: it names where a formal system stops. And marking that boundary precisely turns out to be its own deep subject. Stack an oracle on top of a system and you get a strictly harder problem; stack one on that and you get a harder one again — an entire ordered hierarchy of unsolvability, mapped in fine detail. The gap past computation isn't blank space. It has structure, and formalists spend careers charting it.

    Final Question Human beings can describe entities that exceed every Turing-computable procedure.

    Does this reveal a deeper mathematical reality beyond computation, or does it merely reveal the ability of language to speak about things no formal system can generate?

    Interpretation Layer: When Syntax Becomes “Objects”

    The Rules of the Grid

    The Game of Life is a cellular automaton built by mathematician John Conway. Four rules make up the whole system — and it is enough to produce patterns that move, breed, and even compute.

    The Game takes place on an unbounded grid of square cells. Each cell is either Alive or Dead. The grid updates every "generation" based on four simple rules regarding its 8 neighbors:

    • Underpopulation: A live cell with < 2 live neighbors dies.
    • Survival: A live cell with 2 or 3 live neighbors lives on.
    • Overpopulation: A live cell with > 3 live neighbors dies.
    • Reproduction: A dead cell with exactly 3 live neighbors becomes alive.

    Syntax and the Observer's Eye

    The Game of Life draws a clean line between syntax and interpretation. At the level of formal description, the system is nothing but a grid of cells and a handful of local update rules.

    The rules contain no reference to objects, motion, purpose, or meaning.

    Yet observers quickly begin to speak about gliders moving across the screen, guns firing projectiles, and even machines capable of computation.

    This raises a philosophical question: are such structures discovered within the formal system, or are they imposed by the observer?

    Questions to Consider

    • If nobody had ever named a glider, would gliders still exist?
    • Is a glider part of the formal system itself, or part of our interpretation of the system?
    • Why do independent observers identify the same higher-level structures?
    • Does emergence reveal new entities, or merely new ways of describing old ones?
    • At what point does a pattern become an object?

    Where Does "Emergence" Live?

    From a Platonist perspective, the glider is not merely a useful description. It is an objective mathematical structure. Once the rules are fixed, the existence and behavior of gliders follow necessarily. Observers don't invent these patterns any more than they invent prime numbers; they discover them. That independent observers pick out the same structures, prove the same properties, and build the same computational devices is read as evidence that the patterns are objectively real.

    But the formalist asks a different set of questions:

    • Is the glider actually "in" the system, or is it a compression scheme used by the human mind to avoid tracking thousands of individual cell updates?
    • If the system is just pure syntax, isn't "emergence" simply a name for our inability to avoid interpreting rule-generated structure as objects?

    Rather than treat the glider as a basic part of the system, the formalist treats it as a higher-level description — one that arises where the rules meet an observer's way of organizing what they see. "Emergence" names that gap: the moment a rule-system's output is easier to describe in a new vocabulary (objects, motion) than in the original one (cells flipping on and off).

    Calling it a description doesn't make it flimsy. The glider is a real invariant of the dynamics: it survives, it moves at a fixed speed, you can count them and predict them. And they compose. Line gliders up right and they act as logic gates; wire enough gates together and the grid computes anything a computer can. Four rules, and the emergent vocabulary carries all the way up to universal computation. The formalist reading doesn't explain emergence away — it says exactly where emergence lives, and then studies it.

    The Philosophical Layer

    This brings us back to the core tension. A formal system is just a mechanical generator of strings or states. The moment we say it "computes," "simulates," or "contains objects," we have stepped outside the formalism and added an interpretation layer.

    Naming emergence this way doesn't flatten it. It opens a question worth chasing: not just what the rules are, but why simple rule-sets so reliably push observers into inventing richer vocabularies just to keep up.

    Final Question When we say that a glider "exists," are we describing the formal system, or ourselves?

    Formal Systems & Semantics

    The Foundation

    In the philosophy of mathematics, Realism (or Platonism) is the view that mathematical objects—like numbers, sets, and geometric shapes—exist independently of human thought, and that mathematics is the discovery of universal truths about them.

    In contrast, Formalism holds that mathematics isn't about discovering external truths at all. It is the study of formal systems: rule-governed games played with symbols. Meaning and truth aren't inside the strings; they're a layer we add afterward. That isn't a way of calling mathematics empty — the systems themselves are the subject, and they run deep. What a system can reach, what stays forever beyond it, and how systems nest inside stronger ones are exact, hard-won questions. The five chapters are a tour of that structure.

      Every move you made was pure syntax — no move ever asked what M, I, or U mean. And yet you hit a wall: MU stayed out of reach no matter how you tried.

Hold onto that feeling: it is exactly the shape of the deeper limits — Gödel's and Turing's — waiting in the chapters ahead.

      Reverse Mathematics

      Recovering Rules from a Record

      Running the Machine Backwards

      Every chapter so far ran mathematics forwards: fix the axioms, turn the crank of the rules, read off the theorems. The MIU strings, the Gödel unrolling, the Turing tape — all of it moved in that one direction, from rules to consequences.

      This chapter runs the same machine backwards. You're not handed the rules. You're handed a record — a run of states, the output, the way things actually went — and your job is to infer a rule-system that could have produced it. Not deriving consequences from given rules, but recovering the rules from their consequences, and keeping whichever system reproduces the record with the fewest, simplest rules.

      In the formalist spirit, this is pure syntax. The record is a sequence of strings, nothing more. What those strings mean is a separate layer you can bolt on or leave off — read them as a physical universe, as a language, as a stream of data, or as nothing at all. The backward move runs the same either way. That's the point: it isn't a fact about matter, it's a move you can make on any generated record.

      Given the output, which rules could have written it?

      Physics happens to be the loudest reading of this. Science is never handed the laws; it only ever gets the data, and it looks for rules that compress the data — one compact F = ma standing in for where every apple that ever fell landed. A theory earns its keep when it packs a huge record into a few rules without losing predictive grip. That's a real job, and a hard one: the formalist isn't giving up on truth, just trading it for something you can actually check. But strip the physics away and the same game is still there: a record, and the search for what could have generated it.

      The sandbox below makes the move literal. A source is already running under one hidden rule-set. Watch its record unfold and rule out every candidate that couldn't have produced it — recovering the rules from the record, one state at a time. Call the source a universe if you like; it doesn't have to be one.

      A note on the name. In logic, "reverse mathematics" is also a specific research program (Friedman, Simpson) that asks which axioms are strictly necessary to prove a given theorem — reasoning from a result back to what it requires. The spirit is the same, but here we use the phrase in its looser sense: running the derivation machine backwards, from a record of output to the rules that could have generated it.

      When Models Reach Their Limits

      Now push it one step further.

      Suppose we observe something our current model doesn't describe, yet nothing it directly contradicts. Just a gap in the model? Or a hint about the limits of formal description itself?

      Gödel's theorem offers an analogy worth taking seriously. Expressive systems throw off statements they can't settle from inside. Whether that ever bites in physics is a separate question — but it points at a possibility:

      Could there be aspects of reality that continually escape complete formal capture, not because the universe is inconsistent, but because formal description itself has limits?

      A Platonist may interpret such situations as evidence that our current framework is only a partial glimpse of a larger mathematical reality.

      The formalist is inclined to ask different questions:

      • Can there ever be a complete description of reality?
      • If a final theory existed, how would we recognize it?
      • Will every sufficiently rich model eventually encounter new forms of incompleteness?
      • What is the meaning of a limitation that appears in every formal framework we construct?
      • Are the boundaries we encounter properties of reality, or properties of our methods of description?

      These questions do not reject meaning or depth. They relocate them.

      Where Does Meaning Live?

      The Platonic view often places meaning in an external mathematical reality. Formal systems are valuable because they approximate that reality.

      The formalist perspective asks us to consider another possibility:

      Perhaps meaning does not reside beyond the formal system, but in the relationship between observations, models, and the limits of those models.

      This doesn't make mathematics or physics smaller. It makes them more open.

      Instead of asking what ultimate truths exist beyond our theories, we can investigate why certain descriptions succeed, why others fail, how complexity emerges from simple rules, and whether the idea of a final complete description is itself meaningful.

      The strongest form of the view:

      If all we ever have are observations, and all our explanations take the form of formal models, then "truth" may never be something we discover outside those models.

      What we can discuss are observations, consistencies, derivations, predictions, failures, and refinements. The moment we speak about truths beyond every possible model, we have stepped into philosophy rather than physics.

      That doesn't make those discussions invalid — they're among the ones most worth having. The formalist's contribution is just to mark where the mathematics ends and the philosophical assumptions begin. And it points out that the limits Gödel found need not only gesture at a higher realm. They can just as well open onto a richer understanding of models, meaning, and explanation themselves.
