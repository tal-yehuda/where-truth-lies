Project Proposal: Syntax vs Semantics

This project is an interactive website that explores how mathematical rules (syntax) relate to human interpretation and meaning (semantics). It focuses on computability and the foundations of mathematics through five interactive chapters. A guiding principle of this work is the deliberate use of the tension between different interpretations of mathematics (such as formalist and realist perspectives) as a framework for exploration rather than resolution.

Each chapter uses a specialized sandbox to ground abstract ideas in concrete mechanics. The MIU string-rewriting system introduces the nature of formal systems. A logical calculator illustrates Gödel's incompleteness results in an accessible way. A Turing machine simulator demonstrates the limits of formal computation and specification. The Game of Life explores how complex patterns emerge from simple rules and how interpretation depends on the observer. Finally, a physics simulation connects these ideas to the role of modeling in scientific understanding.

Overall, the project uses hands-on interaction to help users reflect on how meaning and interpretation arise from formal structures.

About the Project

Goals and Main Outcomes

The main goal of this project is to make difficult concepts in mathematics easier to understand, and help develop philosophical thinking and reasoning in the context of mathematics. It covers topics like Gödel's Incompleteness, Turing's Halting Problem, and the tension between Formalism and Realism. The final product is a digital learning tool that uses interactive games to help users easily grasp the limits of mathematical systems, ultimately asking them to consider where mathematical truth actually lies.

Design Choices

The main design choice for this project is "experience first, interpretation second." Each of the five chapters requires the user to interact with a specific formal model—a string-rewriting game, a logical calculator, a Turing machine, a cellular automaton, and a physics simulation—before reading the essays explaining them. This hands-on structure is meant to clearly show the difference between blindly following mechanical rules and assigning higher-level meaning to those rules.

Challenges & AI Contributions

The main intellectual challenge when writing the content was navigating the tension between different philosophies without falling into extremes—such as sliding into pure Platonism when discussing Gödel, or pure Formalism when discussing physics. It requires effort to keep an open mind when dealing with such absolute mathematical boundaries, but that effort is what produced the even-handed treatment we aimed for throughout the site.

On the technical side, AI tools were used to help write the frontend code (HTML, CSS, and JavaScript), fix bugs in the interactive sandboxes, and clarify the text. However, we made all final design and conceptual decisions to ensure the project met its educational goals. We are especially proud of the original physics game, which is both a fun sandbox and a powerful philosophical analogy, as well as the Gödel proof re-roller and logical calculator, as building them is what finally made Gödel's reasoning click for us.

Truth, Proof, and the Illusion of Mathematical Existence

Gödel’s incompleteness theorem is often presented as a metaphysical turning point: a formal system cannot capture all “truths” about arithmetic, therefore mathematical truth must exist beyond formal proof. But this reading already commits to a strong assumption—that there is a fixed notion of truth waiting to be compared against derivations. If we remove that assumption and focus only on consistency, derivability, and modeling, Gödel’s construction becomes less a statement about a hidden realm and more a case study in a broader tension: the relationship between formal systems and the meanings we project onto them.

A formal system, in its bare form, contains no semantics. It consists of symbols and rules for transforming those symbols. Meaning enters only when an external perspective interprets those symbols as referring to numbers, physical states, or anything else. From this angle, the idea that a system “fails to capture truth” is already a step outside the system. Inside the system, there is only what can or cannot be derived. Nothing in the formalism itself distinguishes between “describing reality,” “being true,” or “being incomplete.” Those are interpretive layers we place on top.

This shift matters because it changes what we take incompleteness to be. Instead of viewing it as evidence of a Platonic domain of mathematical facts, we can view it as evidence that any sufficiently expressive system of rules will inevitably interact with its own descriptive limits. When a system becomes powerful enough to encode its own reasoning processes, questions about its behavior begin to refer back to the system itself. At that point, the system is no longer just describing external structure; it is also partially describing its own capacity for description.

Platonism vs Formalism: Where Gödel Actually Leaves Us

The Platonist interpretation posits a completed mathematical structure in which all statements have fixed status independent of our descriptions. This view is powerful and coherent: it explains why mathematical practice feels discovery-like, and why independent systems often seem to converge on the same results. But it introduces a strong ontological commitment—the existence of a fully determinate mathematical universe—that is not itself a consequence of formal reasoning. It is a philosophical choice that elevates one interpretation of formal success into a claim about what mathematics ultimately is.

Seen this way, Gödel’s construction is not a portal to metaphysical truth but a clean illustration of a more general phenomenon: expressive systems can generate statements that reflect their own limitations. This is not unique to logic. It appears whenever a language, model, or theory becomes rich enough to refer to its own mechanisms.

The important point is not that there exists a privileged “truth” outside the system, but that internal expressivity and self-reference create unavoidable boundaries in what can be settled within a fixed rule set.

2. Symbols and primitives

We assume a base theory T (e.g. Peano Arithmetic).

  • ⌜x⌝ : Gödel number of expression x
  • Sub(a,b) : Computes the Gödel number of the formula that results from taking the formula with Gödel number a and substituting numeral b into its free variable.
  • Proof(p,f) : p encodes a valid proof of formula f
  • Prov(f) := ∃p Proof(p,f)

3. Definitions

Let:

  1. Prov(f) := ∃p Proof(p,f) "Formula f is provable (there exists a proof p for f)"
  2. ϕ(x) := ¬Prov(x) "The formula with Gödel number x is unprovable"
  3. ψ(x) := ϕ(Sub(x,x)) "If you substitute x into its own formula, the result is unprovable"
  4. n := ⌜ψ⌝ "Let n be the Gödel number of the formula ψ"
  5. G := ψ(n) "Construct G by applying ψ to its own Gödel number n"

4. Pure Derivation (Line-by-Line)

  1. ϕ(x) := ¬Prov(x) (Define property ϕ as "is unprovable")
  2. ψ(x) := ϕ(Sub(x,x)) (Define property ψ as applying ϕ to self-substitution)
  3. n := ⌜ψ⌝ (Let n be the literal Gödel number of formula ψ)
  4. G := ψ(n) (Construct G by evaluating ψ with its own Gödel number)
  5. G = ϕ(Sub(n,n)) (Expand ψ using its definition from step 3)
  6. G = ¬Prov(Sub(n,n)) (Expand ϕ using its definition from step 2)
  7. Sub(n,n) = ⌜ψ(n)⌝ (Mechanically, Sub(n,n) computes the Gödel number of ψ(n))
  8. Sub(n,n) = ⌜G⌝ (Since G is exactly ψ(n), their Gödel numbers are identical)
  9. Therefore:
    G = ¬Prov(⌜G⌝) (Substitute the string ⌜G⌝ for Sub(n,n) into step 6)

5. Final Gödel Sentence

G ↔ ¬Prov(⌜G⌝)

Platonism vs Formalism: Where Gödel Actually Leaves Us

The Platonist reading of Gödel's theorem interprets the unprovability of G as evidence of a world beyond the system. Since we "know" G is true, mathematical truth must extend beyond mechanical proof.

But a rigorous formalist perspective clarifies something vital: the Gödel numbering we used above is merely a technical apparatus. It forces us to ask: Does the formal system actually possess an internal truth predicate?

Analyzing G From the Outside

Let us analyze the situation purely structurally. If we assume system A is consistent, then G cannot be proven. However, we also know that ¬G cannot be proven (otherwise A would be inconsistent).

Therefore, G does not logically follow from the axioms. It is strictly independent. You could create a perfectly consistent mathematical universe by adding G as a new axiom, and an equally consistent universe by adding ¬G as a new axiom.

This does not mean truth is arbitrary, but rather that the system does not uniquely determine truth-values for all sentences. Inside the bare syntax, there is no notion of truth—only derivability. Truth arises only relative to an interpretation (a model).

We can step outside the system and say "G is true," but we only do this by mapping the symbols to standard arithmetic in our minds. By doing so, we introduce a distinction between mechanical derivability and truth-in-a-structure.

Gödel's theorem does not just "separate" truth from provability—it shows that any attempt to fully unify them inside a single system breaks in a very specific, structural way. The system can reflect on itself, but it can never completely internalize its own truth without incompleteness arising.

In this way, Gödel does not prove Platonism, nor does it force strict formalism. Instead, it proves that no sufficiently rich formal system can close the gap between syntax and semantics. Philosophical interpretations—whether Platonist, Formalist, or Structuralist—are simply our different ways of deciding what that uncapturable "truth" ultimately means.

Computability

The Limit of Algorithms

From Formal Systems to Machines

In the previous sections, we treated mathematics as a formal system: a collection of symbols together with rules that determine which strings may be transformed into which others. Proofs were viewed not as discoveries of meaning, but as legal sequences of symbolic manipulations.

Computability extends this perspective. Instead of asking which strings can be derived, it asks which tasks can be carried out by a purely mechanical procedure. To study this question, mathematicians developed one of the most influential abstractions in the history of mathematics and computer science: the Turing Machine.

The Turing Machine

A Turing Machine is an idealized computational device consisting of an infinite tape divided into cells, a read/write head, a finite set of internal states, and a collection of rules.

At every step, the machine reads the current symbol, consults its transition rules, writes a symbol, changes state, and moves left or right.

Nothing in this description involves intelligence, understanding, creativity, or meaning. A Turing Machine simply follows rules.

From a formalist perspective, this is exactly what makes it interesting. A Turing Machine is not fundamentally different from the formal systems encountered earlier. The primary difference is that a proof system rewrites formulas while a Turing Machine rewrites tape configurations.

When viewed formally, computation is simply another form of derivation. A computer executing software is not fundamentally different from a formal proof system generating theorems. Both are engines of syntax. Meaning enters only when an observer interprets the resulting symbols as numbers, images, predictions, or answers.

The Question of Decidability

Once computation is viewed as mechanical rule-following, a natural question arises: Can every well-defined question be answered by a mechanical procedure?

If a problem is clearly stated and precise enough, shouldn't there exist some sufficiently clever method for solving it? Computability theory revealed that this intuition is false. Some questions are not merely difficult. They are undecidable. No algorithm can answer them in all cases.

The Halting Problem

Suppose we are given a program (P) and an input (I). We ask: Will program (P) eventually stop when run on input (I), or will it continue forever?

At first glance it appears reasonable. Yet Alan Turing proved that no universal algorithm can correctly answer it for every possible program and input. No matter how sophisticated the algorithm becomes, there will always exist programs for which its prediction fails.

Why The Halting Problem Appears

The proof resembles Gödel's incompleteness theorem. Both constructions exploit self-reference.

Machine D (Input: Itself)

Imagine a machine H that perfectly solves the Halting Problem.
Now construct a new program D:

1. Run H on Machine D.
2. If H says "D will Halt" → Enter infinite loop!
3. If H says "D runs forever" → Halt immediately!
What happens when D is given itself as input?

Any answer produces a contradiction. The assumption that H exists must therefore be false. The obstacle appears when a system attempts to mechanically predict the behavior of systems like itself.

The Missing Philosophical Tension

A visitor will naturally think: "Fine. No Turing machine can solve the Halting Problem. But I can still imagine such a machine."

But notice what has actually been ruled out. The machine is not physically impossible. The machine is not logically contradictory. You can describe it perfectly: Input a program and an input. Output HALTS or DOES NOT HALT. Always correct.

The description is perfectly meaningful. Yet no Turing machine can instantiate it. So what is the ontological status of this thing?

The Platonist Response

A Platonist is often comfortable saying: The machine is impossible as a Turing machine, but the function it computes still exists. The halting relation itself is perfectly well-defined. For every program P and input I, there is a fact of the matter. The inability of a Turing machine to determine all such facts does not eliminate the facts themselves. From this perspective, computability theory is not discovering the limits of truth. It is discovering the limits of a particular method of accessing truth. The Platonic realm contains more than algorithms can reach.

The Formalist's Counterquestion

The formalist immediately asks: In what sense does this "machine" exist? Not: Can we build it? but: What does existence mean here? The machine cannot be specified as a Turing machine. It cannot be generated by the formal system being discussed. So what exactly is being asserted when someone says: "It exists." Is existence being confused with description? Is the ability to imagine an object enough to grant it ontological status?

The Human Problem

This leads to an even stranger question. Humans routinely discuss objects that no Turing machine can realize. We discuss halting oracles, infinite sets, arbitrary real numbers, and complete truth predicates. The concepts themselves appear perfectly understandable. Yet the systems we study cannot generate them.

So where did the concepts come from?

The Platonist sees this as evidence that human thought is reaching toward a genuine mathematical reality. The fact that we can meaningfully refer to non-computable objects suggests that mathematics is not exhausted by computation. The oracle cannot be computed. Yet it can be understood. For the Platonist, understanding is not identical to computation. The mind is capable of referring to structures that no algorithm can fully capture.

The formalist is less willing to grant this conclusion. The formalist asks: Have we actually understood the oracle? Or have we merely attached a label to a gap in our formal machinery? Saying "A machine that always solves the Halting Problem" may not represent genuine understanding. It may merely be a placeholder. After all, one can also say: "A machine that answers every question." The sentence is grammatically meaningful. That does not mean the object itself has been coherently specified.

The formalist therefore treats the oracle not as a discovered entity but as a useful boundary marker. It identifies where a formal system stops. Nothing more needs to be assumed.

Final Question Human beings can describe entities that exceed every Turing-computable procedure.

Does this reveal a deeper mathematical reality beyond computation, or does it merely reveal the ability of language to speak about things no formal system can generate?

Interpretation Layer: When Syntax Becomes “Objects”

The Rules of the Grid

The Game of Life is a cellular automaton created by mathematician John Conway. Despite having only four simple rules, it can generate surprisingly complex behavior.

The Game takes place on an unbounded grid of square cells. Each cell is either Alive or Dead. The grid updates every "generation" based on four simple rules regarding its 8 neighbors:

  • Underpopulation: A live cell with < 2 live neighbors dies.
  • Survival: A live cell with 2 or 3 live neighbors lives on.
  • Overpopulation: A live cell with > 3 live neighbors dies.
  • Reproduction: A dead cell with exactly 3 live neighbors becomes alive.

Syntax and the Observer's Eye

Conway's Game of Life provides a striking example of the distinction between syntax and interpretation. At the level of formal description, the system consists only of a grid of cells and a collection of local update rules.

The rules contain no reference to objects, motion, purpose, or meaning.

Yet observers quickly begin to speak about gliders moving across the screen, guns firing projectiles, and even machines capable of computation.

This raises a philosophical question: are such structures discovered within the formal system, or are they imposed by the observer?

Questions to Consider

  • If nobody had ever named a glider, would gliders still exist?
  • Is a glider part of the formal system itself, or part of our interpretation of the system?
  • Why do independent observers identify the same higher-level structures?
  • Does emergence reveal new entities, or merely new ways of describing old ones?
  • At what point does a pattern become an object?

Where Does "Emergence" Live?

From a Platonist perspective, the glider is not merely a useful description. It is an objective mathematical structure. Once the rules of the Game of Life are fixed, the existence and behavior of gliders follow necessarily. Human observers do not invent these patterns any more than they invent prime numbers; they merely discover them. The remarkable fact that independent observers identify the same structures, prove the same properties, and construct the same computational devices is often taken as evidence that these patterns possess a form of objective mathematical reality.

But the formalist asks a different set of questions:

  • Is the glider actually "in" the system, or is it a compression scheme used by the human mind to avoid tracking thousands of individual cell updates?
  • If the system is just pure syntax, isn't "emergence" simply a name for our inability to avoid interpreting rule-generated structure as objects?

Rather than treating the glider as a primitive constituent of the formal system, the formalist treats it as a higher-level description that emerges from the interaction between the system's rules and the observer's way of organizing information. We call it "emergence" when a formal system generates outputs that are easier to describe with a new vocabulary (objects, motion) than with the original rules (cell state updates).

The Philosophical Layer

This brings us back to the core tension. A formal system is just a mechanical generator of strings or states. The moment we say it "computes," "simulates," or "contains objects," we have stepped outside the formalism and added an interpretation layer.

By recognizing this, we do not destroy the beauty of emergence. Instead, we open a new philosophical horizon: we can study not just the rules themselves, but the extraordinary capacity of simple rule sets to force observers into inventing new vocabularies just to keep up.

Final Question When we say that a glider "exists," are we describing the formal system, or ourselves?

Formal Systems & Semantics

The Foundation

In the philosophy of mathematics, Realism (or Platonism) is the view that mathematical objects—like numbers, sets, and geometric shapes—exist independently of human thought, and that mathematics is the discovery of universal truths about them.

In contrast, Formalism is the view that mathematics is not about discovering external truths at all. Instead, it is the study of formal systems: rule-governed games played with symbols. From this perspective, mathematics simply generates strings of symbols according to strict mechanical rules, and any "meaning" or "truth" is just an interpretive layer we choose to project onto those strings afterward.

    Physics as Reverse Mathematics

    The Universe as a System

    Physics Through a Formalist Lens

    Let us begin with a strong assumption:

    The universe, and everything that exists within it, is governed by a consistent formal system.

    If we truly adopt this assumption, then there is no room for "things that exist" outside the rules. Every event, every particle, every observation would already be a consequence of the system. There would be no external realm of truth waiting beyond the axioms.

    At first glance this may seem like a radically different position from Platonism, but notice something surprising: it ends discussion just as quickly. If reality simply is a formal system, then every observation is already accounted for by definition. The statement is as complete and assertive as the Platonic claim that reality is grounded in a fully determined mathematical structure. In both cases, there is little left to investigate philosophically.

    Physics traditionally proceeds differently.

    Rather than beginning with a claim about what reality ultimately is, physics begins with observation. In the spirit of "I think, therefore I am", one might say:

    "I observe, therefore there is something to explain."

    Or more practically:

    "I model, therefore I understand."

    A model is not reality. A model is a formal structure that successfully organizes observations. Newtonian mechanics is not reality itself, yet it remains one of the most successful models ever constructed because it consistently describes an enormous range of phenomena.

    This shift is subtle but important. The formalist need not ask whether a model captures an ultimate truth. Instead, the question becomes:

    How well does a model organize and predict what we observe?

    In this view, a physical law is essentially a mathematical compression scheme. Instead of recording the exact position of every falling apple in history (an uncompressed list of raw data), we compress all those observations into a single, compact syntactic rule: F = ma. A formal system in physics is considered "viable" if it can compress vast amounts of observational data into a small set of axioms and transition rules without losing predictive accuracy.

    Physics as Reverse Mathematics

    Here is the cleanest way to say what physics is really doing. Ordinary mathematics runs forwards: fix your axioms, turn the crank of the rules, and read off the theorems they generate. Every derivation you have done so far — the MIU strings, the Gödel unrolling — ran in that direction.

    Physics runs the same machine backwards. You are never handed the axioms. You are handed the theorems — the observations, the data, the way the world actually went — and your task is to figure out a system that could have produced them. That is the move worth taking seriously: not deriving consequences from given rules, but inferring the rules from their consequences, then keeping whichever system compresses the most observation into the fewest laws.

    The sandbox below makes this literal. A universe is already running under one hidden law. You watch it evolve and rule out every candidate that could not have produced what you saw — reverse-engineering the rules from the record, one observation at a time.

    When Models Reach Their Limits

    Now push the picture one step further.

    Suppose we observe a phenomenon that is not described by our current model, yet never directly contradicts it. Is this merely a gap in the model? Or is it revealing something deeper about the nature of formal descriptions themselves?

    Gödel's incompleteness theorem provides an intriguing analogy. It shows that sufficiently expressive formal systems encounter statements that cannot be settled from within the system itself. Whether or not this phenomenon appears in physics is a separate question, but it suggests a broader philosophical possibility:

    Could there be aspects of reality that continually escape complete formal capture, not because the universe is inconsistent, but because formal description itself has limits?

    A Platonist may interpret such situations as evidence that our current framework is only a partial glimpse of a larger mathematical reality.

    The formalist is inclined to ask different questions:

    • Can there ever be a complete description of reality?
    • If a final theory existed, how would we recognize it?
    • Will every sufficiently rich model eventually encounter new forms of incompleteness?
    • What is the meaning of a limitation that appears in every formal framework we construct?
    • Are the boundaries we encounter properties of reality, or properties of our methods of description?

    These questions do not reject meaning or depth. They relocate them.

    Where Does Meaning Live?

    The Platonic view often places meaning in an external mathematical reality. Formal systems are valuable because they approximate that reality.

    The formalist perspective asks us to consider another possibility:

    Perhaps meaning does not reside beyond the formal system, but in the relationship between observations, models, and the limits of those models.

    This does not make mathematics or physics less profound. In many ways it makes them more philosophically open.

    Instead of asking what ultimate truths exist beyond our theories, we can investigate why certain descriptions succeed, why others fail, how complexity emerges from simple rules, and whether the idea of a final complete description is itself meaningful.

    The sharpest edge of this viewpoint is the following:

    If all we ever have are observations, and all our explanations take the form of formal models, then "truth" may never be something we discover outside those models.

    What we can discuss are observations, consistencies, derivations, predictions, failures, and refinements. The moment we speak about truths beyond every possible model, we have stepped into philosophy rather than physics.

    That does not make such discussions invalid. On the contrary, they are some of the deepest discussions available. The formalist contribution is simply to make explicit where the mathematics ends and where the philosophical assumptions begin. It reminds us that the limits revealed by Gödel need not only point toward a higher realm; they can also point toward a richer understanding of models, meaning, and explanation themselves.
